Abstract
------

Pruning at Initialization (PaI) is a technique in neural network optimization characterized by the proactive elimination of weights before the network's training on designated tasks. This innovative strategy potentially reduces the costs for training and inference, significantly advancing computational efficiency. A key factor leading to PaI's effectiveness is that it considers the saliency of weights in an untrained network, and prioritizes the trainability and optimization potential of the pruned subnetworks. Recent methods can effectively prevent the formation of hard-to-optimize networks, e.g. through iterative adjustments at each network layer. However, this way often results in large-scale discrete optimization problems, which could make PaI further challenging. This paper introduces a novel method, called DPaI, that involves a differentiable optimization of the pruning mask. DPaI adopts a dynamic and adaptable pruning process, allowing easier optimization processes and better solutions. More importantly, our differentiable formulation enables readily use of the existing rich body of efficient gradient-based methods for PaI. Our empirical results demonstrate that DPaI significantly outperforms current state-of-the-art PaI methods on various architectures, such as Convolutional Neural Networks and Vision-Transformers.

My Contribution
------

In this work, I proposed a differentiable optimization approach for discovering [Node Path Balance (NPB)](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2023/hash/fd5013ea0c3f96931dec77174eaf9d80-Abstract-Conference.html) space networks. This idea was motivated by the limitations of existing methods, which rely on discrete optimization—often slow and sub-optimal for solving this NP-hard problem. To address this, I reformulated the problem into a differentiable framework. By assigning a learnable score to each parameter, I identified the sparse subnetwork using a Top-K selection function, then calculated the number of effective nodes and paths.

The main challenge lay in computing gradients to update the score parameters in a way that maximizes the number of effective nodes and paths. I leveraged the straight-through estimator to effectively compute gradients for the number of effective paths. For effective nodes, which was a more difficult objective, I calculated their count by estimating the number of incoming and outgoing paths via the derivative of effective paths with respect to each node. I also conducted a theoretical analysis, proving that my method guarantees progressive improvement—each optimization step results in an increase in the number of effective nodes or paths.

I was also responsible for implementing the proposed method, which posed significant challenges due to the need for deep modifications within the core layers of neural networks and the handling of big number calculations. Ultimately, I developed a flexible framework that integrates the proposed approach into any neural network architecture without requiring changes to the model's structure. This greatly enhances the versatility and usability of the method.

I conducted extensive experiments to demonstrate that our approach produces more optimal sparse networks, outperforming all existing baselines while significantly reducing pruning time. Additionally, I explored the node-path balance trade-off and showed that a wide range of hyperparameter settings within this balance zone still yields high-performing sparse networks. This highlights the robustness of the proposed method and its insensitivity to hyperparameter choices.
